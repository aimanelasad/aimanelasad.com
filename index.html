About me

I’m Aiman — a physicist by training and an industrial AI researcher by profession. I work at Mercedes-Benz AG in Sindelfingen, where I did my doctoral research with the University of Stuttgart on how vehicle software updates affect production. The facts are in the CV; this page is about what drives me.

The short answer is curiosity. I ask a lot of questions — probably more than is comfortable for the people around me — because I want to understand things from the ground up, not just know that they work. One of my favourite mathematicians put it better than I can:

“Children are not afraid to pose basic questions that may embarrass us, as adults, to ask.”

— Roger Penrose, The Emperor’s New Mind (1989)

That is also what fascinates me about AI: the interplay between models and data. I like to dig into different model architectures — from classical machine learning to transformers and agentic LLM systems — and understand what each of them can and cannot do. Just as much, I enjoy the less glamorous part: preparing the data. Cleaning, structuring and enriching raw data so that it becomes fertile ground for a model is, in my experience, where most of the output is decided — and getting that right for a specific goal is the part of the work I find most rewarding.

Away from the screen you will most likely find me on my motorcycle. I love riding: the landscape opening up, the headwind, the feeling of being part of the scenery rather than looking at it. When I’m not riding, I cook, take photographs, look after plants and read.

I also have a soft spot for classic cars — the IAA 2025 in Munich was a highlight of last year.

Most of all, though, I owe a great deal to the people around me. Supervisors, colleagues, mentors and friends have shaped how I think and who I am — far more than any book or course.

Projects

Open-source projects from my spare time. All code is on GitHub (opens in a new tab).

gpu-kernel-lab

Roofline-driven CUDA kernel optimisation. Four workloads — 2D stencil, reduction, softmax and SGEMM — are tuned in documented steps, each measured against the hardware ceiling it is bound by (SGEMM reaches 97% of cuBLAS on an RTX A2000 Laptop GPU). Hypotheses that turned out wrong stay in the code and are labelled as such — the method is the point, not the final numbers.

Source on GitHub (opens in a new tab)

torch-roofline

What is your PyTorch model actually bound by? A per-operator roofline profiler: it runs a model once, intercepts every operator, records time, FLOPs and bytes moved, and places each op on the measured roofline of your device. Reports memory- vs compute-bound ops, fusion candidates and whether lower precision, torch.compile or bigger batches are the right lever. CUDA, Apple MPS and CPU.

Source on GitHub (opens in a new tab)
